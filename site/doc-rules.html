Rules

The source, for people and agents alike: docs/rules.xml

Rules are standards: they keep a schema consistent and fit for what it has to do. The architect sets them up once, on the schema and the levels inside it; the agent reads them before it designs anything and builds inside them, so neither of you repeats them. The rules machinery is shared by every database; the rules about a database belong to its engine (engines.xml): a schema is offered exactly the rules its engine implements, at its version.

Optional

Rules are optional. A schema with nothing linked has no rules; nothing links them for you. Where rules reach from above (global links), no rules here switches every rule off for a schema, a namespace, a family, a group or a table, with a reason. It holds even over locked rules, since it is the choice not to use rules there at all, and a rule linked further down still applies.

Rules made of rules

  • A check looks at a table and says what breaks it: pk.present "Every table has a key".
  • A pack is a rule made of rules, to any depth; a rule may sit in several packs. cdc (CDC-ready) holds the whole pk pack, types.time, mor.modes, cdc.position and cdc.source. Linking a pack brings its tree.
  • Every rule holds hard (enforced: the agent's change is refused) or soft (highlighted only). Each check has a default (Must is hard, Should is soft); you set it per link, and the nearest link that says wins. Every finding says which it is.

Sorted so the catalog stays clear

Every rule has two labels, and the catalog is browsed by them:

Lane: where it appliesExamples
Every databasenothing without a key; one type per column name; money is decimal; snake_case; descriptions; normalization; SCD2
MySQLtables use InnoDB; text is utf8mb4
PostgreSQLtimes are timestamptz; keys are identity columns, not serial
Icebergidentifier fields declared; merge-on-read; a format version of 2 or 3
Between databasescompatibility (a pack per source and destination engine); CDC-ready

Topics within a lane: keys, types, naming, documentation, normalization, history, storage, cdc, compatibility. A check keeps its own lane inside a pack of another: pk.ident is an Iceberg rule in the shared pk pack, and only checks Iceberg schemas.

Linked on the hierarchy

A rule is linked at a level and applies to every table under it:

global (every schema of the workspace)
  └ schema
      └ namespace (and the namespaces inside it)
          └ family (billing__, ungrouped, …)
              └ table
      └ group (tables picked by hand, across namespaces and families)
  • The nearest link wins. A table's rules are found by walking from global down to the table: its namespaces from the outermost, its family, the groups it is in, itself.
  • An exception switches a rule, or one rule inside a pack, off at a level and below, and always says why. The agent is told the reason. Normalization off on a reporting namespace; "no repeating groups" off on one table whose report fixes ten slots, while the rest of Normalization still applies. An exception switches off only the rules it is part of: switching CDC-ready off leaves a Primary keys pack linked on its own running.
  • A locked link cannot be switched off below the level it is linked at: the architect's guardrail ("nothing without a key", locked globally). A lower link of the same rule does not unlock it.
  • A link is checked before it is kept: the rule and the level exist, an exception has a reason and switches off something that reaches every table under it, nothing locked is switched off, and nothing is locked above an exception that would then stop working. The check and the write are one transaction.
  • A new group never replaces one of the same name; changing a group's tables checks its exceptions again.
  • Every change (link_rule, unlink_rule, set_group, remove_group, a goal's link_packs) is an operation in the log, announced live; global links are logged under *. A goal links its packs in one change, all or none.

Goals

A goal says what the schema has to do and links the packs that get it there, by the schema's type:

GoalLinks
Feed it by CDCcdc; lists what must be set on MySQL (binlog ROW and FULL, GTID, grants) or PostgreSQL (logical WAL, a publication and a slot, replica identity)
Build in MySQL and PostgreSQLthe pair's compatibility pack, naming, keys
Move PostgreSQL to Iceberg, Move MySQL to Icebergthe pair's compatibility pack, keys

Before linking, the perspective shows the gap: what the schema still lacks, from a preview that stores nothing.

Compatibility

Each engine maps its types to portable types and back, and every pair of engines is compared through them, never through a map written for the pair (engines.xml, "Moving between engines"). How each type lands is graded exact, widened (bigger, safe), decide (a choice to make: VARCHAR(n) or TEXT), lossy, or no type. Each pair's pack has two checks: nothing lost (Must, breaks on lossy and no type) and every choice made (Should). The same mappings drive conversion between engines, so a check and a conversion never disagree.

  • Diagram, a level: the rules that reach it on the left (quiet lines from wider levels, bright ones linked here or inside it, landing on the gold row they are linked at), the level and what is inside it in the middle, the exceptions on the right in dashed gold. Below: link a rule here (locked, or for every schema), switch one off with a reason, take a link away, and what breaks here as references (@orders.total) that open the table.
  • Diagram, a rule: the packs it sits in, the rules it is made of, where it is linked and switched off, what breaks it, and for a compatibility rule its type map.
  • Catalog: every rule by lane and topic, with what it breaks here, or would break if linked.
  • Goals and Groups.
  • The Table perspective lists the rules on the table, where each comes from, and its findings.

Rules the agent designs

Nobody knows every rule ahead. So the agent proposes them, the system verifies them, you approve them, and the system enforces them.

  1. The agent proposes through the MCP (propose_rules), best from what a schema already does: it reads it (describe) and turns its unwritten conventions into rules, with numbers as evidence ("41 of 42 tables have loaded_at"). A proposal is a new rule written as a sentence, a catalog rule to link, or an exception.
  2. New rules are sentences, run as data, never code:

| Sentence | Example | |---|---| | columns whose name matches must be of a type, required, described or snake_case | {"columns": {"name": {"ends_with": "_at"}, "must": {"of_type": ["timestamptz"]}}} | | every table has a column (required, of a type) | {"has_column": {"column": "loaded_at", "required": true}} | | every table is partitioned by a transform of a column (Iceberg) | {"partitioned_by": {"transform": "identity", "column": "region"}} |

The title you read is made from the sentence itself, so a rule is always exactly what it says.

  1. The system verifies each: written right for the schema's type; not a repeat of a rule or of another proposal; not contradicting a linked rule (a rule that columns could keep only by breaking a linked type rule is a conflict); and what it would break if approved. Proposals for every schema are refused: global rules are yours.
  2. You approve in the Rules perspective's Review: hard or soft, approve or reject. Approving a conflict switches each contradicted rule off there in the same change: its link at that level is taken away, otherwise an exception records that the proposal replaced it (refused if it is locked). An approved new rule joins the workspace's catalog under the next free custom.<n>, linked where it was proposed, with who proposed and approved it; one that names types or transforms holds only on schemas of that engine. The check and the write are one transaction, and the log records the rule, the link and what was switched off.
  3. The system enforces: the agent reads the rules in force (rules, check) and designs within them. Its changes go through the MCP's change, one or more operations as one change, all or none: a change that breaks a hard rule attached to what it touches is refused, with the rule and the reason, and nothing lands; what breaks a soft rule lands and is reported. Hard means hard: a table the change touches must keep every hard rule on it, breaks it had before included, and nothing anywhere may newly break one; untouched tables do not block it. A new or imported schema has no rules; you link them as you choose, hard or soft (typically soft first, hard once the schema keeps them). The agent can never approve, link or switch off a rule itself: that comes back as a proposal.

What breaks what (in the Rules perspective) shows it at a glance: hover a rule and the tables that break it light up, red if it is hard and amber if soft; hover a table and the rules it breaks light up, with what is wrong. Each linked rule is set hard or soft right there.

What rules will cover

  • The agent asks for a kind ("an integer", "money") and gets the standard type; a column of another type is refused or warned, with the standard named.
  • Each kind can say when it applies (columns ending _amount are money; _at are points in time), so existing schemas can be checked too.

Naming

  • case for each kind: tables, columns, views, keys, indexes, PascalCase (TableName), camelCase (tableName), snake_case (table_name), UPPER_SNAKE
  • singular or plural table names
  • prefixes and suffixes by kind or tag: dim_, fact_, stg_, v_ for views, _id for keys
  • templates for constraint and index names: pk_{table}, fk_{table}_{referenced_table}, ix_{table}_{columns}
  • boolean columns start with is_ or has_; time columns end with _at, dates with _on
  • the longest name the engine allows (PostgreSQL 63, MySQL 64), reserved words refused
  • a list of approved abbreviations (qty, amt), others spelled out

Keys and identity

  • every table has a primary key
  • the surrogate key's name and type: id bigint identity, UUID, UUIDv7
  • business keys: required and unique where a table has one
  • composite primary keys allowed or not
  • every foreign key indexed
  • the default on delete / on update for foreign keys
  • Iceberg: identifier fields required, and what a logical relationship must point at

Columns and types

  • types allowed and forbidden: no float or double for money (use decimal), no varchar without a length on MySQL, timestamptz over timestamp on PostgreSQL
  • defaults for new columns: NOT NULL unless said otherwise, string lengths, decimal precision
  • enums: inline (ENUM, PostgreSQL enum types) or lookup tables
  • character set and collation (MySQL utf8mb4 / utf8mb4_0900_ai_ci)

Standard columns

  • audit columns every table carries: created_at, updated_at, created_by
  • soft delete: deleted_at
  • multi-tenant: tenant_id on every table, first in every key
  • their names, types and position, added by the agent when it creates a table

Patterns

What a table tagged with a pattern must have; the agent applies the pattern on request (apply_pattern), and check reports what is missing:

  • SCD Type 1: overwrite in place, updated_at
  • SCD Type 2: surrogate key, business key, the validity columns and their names (valid_from / valid_to, or your own), unique (business key, start of validity)
  • SCD Types 3, 4, 6: previous-value columns, a history table, the combination
  • Star schema: fact tables reference only dimension tables, by surrogate key; dimensions never reference facts
  • Append-only / event tables: no update columns, an event time, partitioned by it on Iceberg
  • Bridge tables: exactly two relationships, their pair unique

Debezium (a rule pack)

For a MySQL or PostgreSQL schema whose changes are captured by Debezium. Add the pack in Standards, to a template or one schema; a CDC pipeline adds it to its source for you.

  • Connector settings, as rules. How types travel is set here, like any rule, and the pipeline writes them out for the connector, so the connector and the designed tables always agree:

| Rule | Shipped default | Other choices | |---|---|---| | decimal.handling.mode | precise (exact decimals) | double, string | | bigint.unsigned.handling.mode | precise (decimal(20,0) on Iceberg; Debezium's own default, long, corrupts values above 2^63 − 1) | long | | time.precision.mode | adaptive_time_microseconds | adaptive, connect | | binary.handling.mode | bytes | base64, base64-url-safe, hex | | TinyInt-to-boolean converter | on, for the columns marked as booleans | off | | zero-date fallback | null | a date you choose, per type or column |

Each choice changes the type mapping at once: set bigint.unsigned.handling.mode to long, and every BIGINT UNSIGNED column maps to long and is flagged.

  • Keys: every captured table has a primary key, or a key Debezium is told to use (message.key.columns); without one there are no reliable updates or deletes downstream.
  • Types Debezium carries badly: BIGINT UNSIGNED flagged unless bigint.unsigned.handling.mode is precise; no FLOAT or DOUBLE for money; DECIMAL flagged unless decimal.handling.mode is precise; TIME within one day; no zero dates in NOT NULL date and time columns.
  • Booleans: TINYINT(1) used as a boolean is marked so the TinyInt-to-boolean converter covers it, and TINYINT(1) used as a number is not.
  • Time: one type for points in time (TIMESTAMP arrives zoned in UTC, DATETIME without a zone); mixing them is flagged.
  • PostgreSQL sources: - Replica identity: a captured table without a primary key needs REPLICA IDENTITY FULL, or its deletes arrive without their data; tables with large values (text, jsonb, bytea) need it too, or an update that leaves them unchanged sends a placeholder (unavailable.value.placeholder) instead. - NUMERIC without a precision arrives with a different scale per value, which Iceberg's decimal(p,s) cannot hold: give it a precision and scale. - INTERVAL arrives as microseconds, with months approximated as average days, unless interval.handling.mode is string; flagged where exactness matters. - TIMETZ arrives as a string; MONEY takes its scale from money.fraction.digits; infinite dates become sentinel values. - More connector settings as rules: interval.handling.mode (shipped string), hstore.handling.mode (shipped json), money.fraction.digits (shipped 2).
  • Names: column names valid for Avro ([A-Za-z_][A-Za-z0-9_]*) when the pipeline uses Avro, or the name adjustment noted; no source column named like a CDC field (__op, __deleted, __source_ts_ms).
  • Enums: a change to ENUM or SET values flagged, since it changes what consumers receive.
  • Schema changes: renames and type changes on captured tables flagged as changes every downstream consumer must take; additions pass.

Documentation

  • a comment on every table, view and column (or only on tables, or on keys)
  • tags and an area on every table

Database views

  • no SELECT *
  • name prefix, comment required
  • materialized views only where allowed; how they are refreshed is documented

Change safety

  • destructive changes (drops, narrowed types, NOT NULL added) need an explicit flag
  • renames instead of drop-and-add
  • after a version is locked, only additive changes (new tables and nullable columns) without a new major version

Size

  • the most columns a table may have, the most indexes, the most tables in an area

The Standards UI

Rules are a product of their own, with a rich UI:

  • Catalog: every rule by category (types, naming, keys, standard columns, patterns, documentation, views, change safety, size), each with its setting and severity, switchable in place.
  • See before you set: every rule shows what it accepts and what it refuses, with live examples that change as you edit it ("orderTotal is refused; expected order_total").
  • Try it on a schema: run a template against an existing or imported schema and see every table and column it would pass or fail, before you adopt it.
  • Compare: two templates, or a template against a schema's copy, side by side.
  • Type standards as a table: each kind of data and its type, per engine, edited like a spreadsheet.
  • Patterns drawn: an SCD Type 2 or star-schema pattern shown as a small diagram of what it adds.
  • History: who changed a standard and when; templates are files, so git keeps them too.
  • With the agent: "make money decimal(19,4) in our PostgreSQL standard" becomes a change shown for you to accept.

How the agent uses them

  • guide gives the agent the schema's type and rules in plain words before it designs anything: "this is an Iceberg schema; tables are snake_case and plural; tables tagged scd2 follow SCD Type 2 with valid_from/valid_to; new tables are merge-on-read and partitioned by day".
  • rules lists each rule, its severity and the tables it applies to; check says what passes and fails.
  • Operations are named and shaped by the rules (a new table gets its audit columns, a new foreign key its templated name and its index), and refused with the rule's name and the expected form when they break one.
