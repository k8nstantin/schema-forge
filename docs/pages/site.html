Open source · Rust · PostgreSQL 18 + pgvector · MCP

Design database schemas with your coding agent, under standards that hold.

SchemaForge is collaborative schema design: your agent designs through MCP while you view and edit the same schema on the canvas, at the same time. Every change, yours and the agent's, is checked against your team's rules, and nothing lands unchecked.

The database at the core

One PostgreSQL. Your design, its rules, its history and its vectors, in one place.

For one person on a laptop or a whole team on a server. The database is designed first and locked, so coding agents fall into it instead of drifting from it.

Real PostgreSQL, bundled

PostgreSQL 18 with pgvector ships inside the app: nothing to install, nothing to configure. Each workspace runs its own private cluster.

One person or a whole team

Point it at a central PostgreSQL server and a team shares one design: the same code, the same schema, only the connection changes.

Metadata and vectors together

Every object, column, relationship and rule sits beside its embedding: one SQL query asks by meaning, by structure and by lineage at once.

Fresh on every change

The log knows exactly what each turn touched, so only that is embedded again: what the agent finds is what is on the canvas.

Guarded by the database

Foreign keys, typed and checked columns, and rules PostgreSQL enforces at every commit, whoever writes: you, your agent or your team.

Grows by versions

Every engine and every feature brings its own namespace and versions; every change adds, and every release upgrades the ones before.

Bring the database you have

Point it at your database. Get to know it.

Read in a live database, a dump or a folder of DDL, and SchemaForge works out what you never wrote down: how it is put together, what depends on what, and what it means. Ask it in plain words, through the canvas or your agent.

Its architecture

Every namespace, table, view, routine, trigger and event, with their keys, indexes and partitions, laid out as one model you can see and walk.

Its relationships

Foreign keys, and what no key declares: which routine reads or writes which table, which view stands on which, what a change would reach.

Its meaning

What each object is for, in sentences kept up to date, and found by meaning: ask for the tables about billing and get them, with the evidence.

Its problems

Tables without keys, money in floats, rules your team wrote that the database breaks today: each a finding with its evidence and its fix.

Collaborative design

Your agent designs through MCP. You see it, and edit it, at the same time.

Ask your agent for a design and watch every table, column and relationship land on the canvas as it makes them. Move in beside it: point at anything, edit it by hand, steer the next prompt. You and the agent work on one live schema, through the same checks, into the same database; each of the agent's answers is a turn you accept or reject whole, and undo takes back any of it.

Every database a plugin

Choose a database first; everything follows it.

The canvas, the MCP, the rules and the store know no database. Each database is an engine: one crate with its versions, types, options, readers and writers, and its own tables, checked by the same conformance kit. How engines plug in, with Apache Iceberg as the example →

MySQL

Dumps, hand-written DDL and the live server read; every kind of object and clause written back for MySQL 8.0 to 26.10. Done.

PostgreSQL and Apache Iceberg

Next, each an engine of its own with its own tables. Iceberg brings the lakehouse: field ids, hidden partitioning, format versions 1 to 3.

Your database

An engine is one crate: its grammar, types and catalogs, and its tables from the owner template. No change to the core.

Convert both ways

Every engine maps into one shared model, so MySQL converts to Iceberg and Iceberg back to MySQL or PostgreSQL, with every type kept, widened or lost named.

Contribute

Learn databases from the inside.

Write an engine and learn a database's grammar, types and catalogs; add a feature's tables and learn relational design and rules enforced at commit; bring a dump that breaks a reader and find the corners of SQL nobody documents. Everyone is welcome.

Most people contribute through a coding agent: point yours at the brief and it reads the whole spec, in order, before anything changes.
